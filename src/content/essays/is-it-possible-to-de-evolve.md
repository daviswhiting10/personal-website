Yesterday I heard a suggestion that we are in the next phase of an evolutionary shift as it pertains to our neurological health. The topic was relevant to what I've been researching so I thought it would be relevant to share here!

The specific concept is called Selective Pressure and it occurs when a species is facing a threat from the environment and must adapt in order to survive. Below are several examples to make this concept easy to understand:

The concept is straightforward and it's worth noting that we're all the descendants of ancestors that outcompeted and adapted correctly in order to survive. We all come from champions and the way our bodies function is about as refined as could be. Is it not motivating to know that we're hardwired for almost every challenge thrown our way?? I think so. But to be fair, most of our adaptations have to do with surviving in wildlife situations where hunting and safety were top concerns. How much of this translates to working in an office with Microsoft teams notifications piling up hour after hour? That's worth reflecting on.

Adaptation is often a slow process that requires several generations, as some die off without offspring while others survive and pass on their favorable genes, which is referred to as being high in fitness. Being high in fitness is frequently correlated with an organism's ability to acquire resources and demonstrate health so that a mating partner will perceive it as a viable option for having healthy and protected offspring. In a world where scarcity is common wherever populations exist in large numbers, defaulting to wanting more resources is perfectly normal for the sake of survival.

But in a time when we have more resources at our fingertips than any other time period of existence, we are in the midst of seeing a new selective pressure develop and the threat comes in the form of neurological disease.

## Anhedonia

One of the terms I heard in relation to this new selective pressure is anhedonia and it refers to "the inability to feel pleasure from an activity that once brought it." Initially I thought the term was equivalent to tolerance but this is not the case. Tolerance refers to needing more stimulus to get the same feeling of reward while anhedonia is the inability to experience the same feeling no matter how much stimulus you receive.

How does this happen?

As you might guess, the primary mechanism here has to do with dopamine, the neurotransmitter responsible for pleasure, motivation, and focus.

The key here is that our ability to experience pleasure via dopamine release requires a "resetting period" where our feeling of dopamine release is sufficient to reward the right circumstances (e.g., doing homework or sticking to a diet). But when dopamine is released from highly stimulating experiences such as gambling, pornography, or eating junk food, the brain considers this to be the new expectation for what reward should feel like.

It's worth seeing the dip as well. Returning to baseline from a splurge of extreme reward can feel depressing.

But what the graph doesn't capture is how frequency plays a role. We not only participate in extreme forms of pleasure, but statistically, many of us (men in this case) do it nearly every day.

The trouble with frequency is that we become used to answering the craving for an experience right away. If you lose the pleasure from the extreme experiences above, trying to participate in delayed gratification will feel nearly impossible.

The new neurological disease is a paradox. Now, because of extreme overabundance, fitness is being challenged. Abundance is being flipped on its head.

## Evolutionary adaptation

It raises the question of what sort of defense mechanisms will arise in the generations that follow us.

There is no inherent defense mechanism that guards against gambling, pornography, or video games the same way that we have hunger or thirst signals. But thinking through this lens might help to begin that evolution within ourselves.

Take hunger. Internally we experience depletion in resources and this manifests in the subjective desire to want food and restore our stores of energy.

In same interview I was listening to, the same logic can be applied to our new neurological dysfunction. In this case, the precursor to engaging in dopamine is boredom. We can't stand boredom. What is happening in the state of boredom is that the brain desiring novel circumstances. In our case, here is the cycle that we all know too well.

Evolutionarily speaking, boredom used to be a signal that we had to do something for survival. Maybe this meant gathering up food, creating a fire, or looking out for danger. This intolerance for boredom served a critical purpose. But now that boredom is the precursor to extreme spikes in dopamine, the mechanism that I'm predicting will become hardwired into our reward pathway is a desire for friction.

I believe that those of us that will thrive above others are those who will, in the moment of experiencing boredom, choose a reward system that has an upfront feeling of friction. I don't know exactly how this all happens neurologically, but my proposed evolution mechanism looks like this.

I imagine that the feeling of checking the phone (and other cheap forms of dopamine) will turn into disgust over time. Conversely, the feeling of friction after boredom will be linked with reward. The question is how quickly can you force it to happen.

An example of friction could simply mean walking outside in silence before switching tasks instead of getting onto the phone. Really, it's anything that resets and keeps your store of dopamine from being spiked out of control.

I know it's tempting to be distracted by my artistic genius but I think this is an extremely important concept to implement and talk about. The stats behind neurological diseases continue to become more dramatic and I can't imagine a scenario in which this behavior change becomes widespread and the outcome isn't great. I'm sure I'm missing a fair amount of understanding in this domain, but I want to keep on looking into it.
