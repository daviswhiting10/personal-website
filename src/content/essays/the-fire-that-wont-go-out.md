As a reminder, we're focusing on understanding the following quote, "The glory of God is man fully alive" from St. Irenaeus with the hopes of understanding what it means to become fully alive ourselves. We started off last week looking into the bare nature of man, concluding that a desire for transcendence is the primary feature that separates humans from other animals. We are not satisfied simply with food and sex for mere survival, but we're always in motion towards things that fill our desire for a deeper and more profound longing. It's the momentary spark that we feel in times of peak moments: Falling in love, mastering a craft, filling a role within our respective social circles … these are the deeper pleasures that keep us driven.

But inevitably, we are left wanting. We all have our own little devils lurking on our shoulders, mentioning words of comparison, of inadequacy, of more. You can always have more. Remember, the transcendent feeling is only a spark. The reward for all that work was merely a conduit for that temporary feeling, rather than a sustainable source. Relationships have moments of passion, but they certainly have valleys of dullness too. C.S. Lewis gave us the amazing quote about how we can turn these things that we hold dear to us into idols, worshipping and expecting results, even unto heartbreak when they fail to satisfy.

We reset back to baseline and begin the chase towards transcendence again. Desire sees an inch and takes a mile.

## From Disciplinary society to Achievement society

Byung-Chul Han, a South Korean-German philosopher, wrote in his book The Burnout Society (a book we will come back to in a later essay) that we have moved away from a disciplinary society where rules and prohibition were key features and into an achievement society where the phrase "yes, we can!" has become the defining message, replacing "should". While this message is one of liberation and freedom to choose, it's also another sneaky form of enslavement.

Han traces this liberation back to Nietzsche's "Sovereign man" which is often attributed with being freed from the confines of religion and authority, saying that there is nothing above him that could tell him who he ought to be because he [is] the sole owner of himself. But Han clarifies that Nietzsche's conclusion is that this freed man is freed to do nothing but work. He has enslaved himself to himself, calling this new human an animal laborans: laboring animal. This ultimate freedom, according to Han has led to a widespread epidemic of mental sickness. He says this of the liberated man:

"The complaint of the depressive individual, 'Nothing is possible', can only occur in a society that thinks, 'Nothing is impossible.'" This illustrates how modern society's emphasis on unlimited potential paradoxically creates mental illness.

He continues: "The achievement-subject stands free from any external instance of domination forcing it to work, much less exploiting it." Yet this freedom ironically becomes another form of bondage, as individuals internalize the pressure to constantly achieve and improve.

In other words, the complete liberation has become a Pandora's box, where the message of "you can do all things" allows us to forget the messages of "You should not do everything". And now instead of madmen and criminals who break the rules, we have a depressed people and losers (his words, used technically) who know nothing about how to satisfy themselves, and in some cases fail to launch.

Desire grows like a cancer. Animal laborans.

## What do we do with uncontrolled desires?

Turning to ancient traditions, we see different attempts at figuring out this dilemma of desire gone astray. In the eastern school of thought, Nirvana represents the state of ultimate peace, where the word literally translates to "Blowing out or extinguishing". The highest goal is to completely rid the self of wants and desires through meditation. According to legend, the only successful practitioners of this method have been Buddha and his early disciples.

The stoic position is similar, although a bit more approachable. Rather than getting rid of all desires, it says to focus on what is within one's reach. This philosophy of living has grown immensely in modern times as we've felt the desire for wealth/status/power/sex grow out of control. In the response of "you can!", the stoic would instruct you to remain independent from external influence and only focus on your immediate surroundings. Epicurus, for example said, "If you wish to make a man wealthy, do not give him more money - take away his desires". It's a compelling school of thought.

In an ideal world, the stoic would be able to not be swayed by emotion and the noise of the outside world while also being in tune with justice and participation with making one's environment better. But this is where I'm going to plant a flag in the ground with disagreement: to be constantly detached from the world and the external world at all times while also engaged in difficult moments that require enduring passion is incompatible. Be it seething anger or unyielding motivation, we all know the feeling of being lit on fire by emotions - where nothing stands in the way. The stoic approach dismisses the value of these innate sources of power that we have within us.

If we cannot tame desire, what do we do?

## Truth

From time to time we encounter people whose love and passion for life is so intense that it leaves a residual sense of conviction in us. Certain people that have a fire lit so intensely that there's nothing that could ever quench the flame. We walk away feeling energized and jealous of whatever dial is turned up in these people.

Desires, emotions, and passions are not meant to be neutralized. Although we have an abundance of desires gone astray, this isn't to say that we need to adopt a Buddhist or stoic approach of dampening our desire. Instead, we should desire more deeply and consider the depths of what our lives could be made for. Coming back to C.S. Lewis, desiring more, not less helps us to raise our eyes above the impulsive, animalistic behaviors of survival and find a target that is worth sacrificing for.

This is all well and good, but how do we find a good target worth going after? Coming back to ancient traditions, truth was the universal ideal to focus on for defining our path. Consider the following thinkers:

Thoreau: "Rather than love, than money, than fame, give me truth"

Plato: "The soul once it has seen the truth will no longer be satisfied with shadow"

Buddha: "The gift of truth (Dhamma) excels all gifts; the taste of truth excels all tastes"

But there is one man in all of history who claimed that He was the truth. Christ, addressing His disciples at the Last Supper says, "I am the way, the truth, and the life" John 14:6.

On the topic of desire, Christ never told His disciples to want less and detach from the world. Instead, He said to increase your desire for God and let that consume you to the point of every other temptation becoming pale in comparison.

Earlier we brought up Byung-Chul Han and his analysis of our unregulated positivity which is leading to a widespread epidemic of depression and burnout. Where the current society offers no guardrails for what you should say yes and no to, Christ is firm in the message of being of service to the world. No, the world is not yours to have. The world is yours to take care of. In conforming to the message of Christ, you become a hero for a cause deeper than hoarding status and goods.

There's a story in the end of the Gospel of Luke where Jesus, after His crucifixion, appears to His disciples on the road to Emmaus and walks for a while with them without being recognized. After leaving, His disciples turned to one another and asked, "were our hearts not burning?" Although they did not recognize Him, they were burning with passion.

This burning is central to St. Irenaeus' description of the fully alive man that we are trying to understand.

Christ as the Truth leads us to not only reject the desires that have been corrupting so much of what is all around us, but also tells us to amplify our desire for something so much more. Is it enough to work a job to make money? Is it enough to get a job title and tell other people about your job title, who will only think about how it makes them feel about their job title? Is it enough to find meaning in sexual partners? How long will these things satisfy?

No matter what your preconceived notions of Christ are, there is more to this life and we all know it. Christ as a path calls us to a higher and more compelling vision.

"You were not made for comfort, you were made for greatness" - Pope Benedict XVI.
