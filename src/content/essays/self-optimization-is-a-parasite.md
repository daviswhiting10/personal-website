Welcome back to The Independent Variable. I'm currently in the midst of a project to investigate what it means for man to be most fully alive, especially in light of today's cultural and technological changes. I have to say that I'm far from an academic and that this writing is for my own sake. But if you are intrigued as I am in figuring out why many of us are suffering deep down despite so much abundance, I invite you to follow along.

## Depression

Today we're talking about happiness…Actually, depression and other neurological sicknesses are going to take the bulk of the writing today, but in the midst of tracking down these, a more clarifying idea of what can help us in our pursuit towards happiness will be carved out. We are Michelangelo carving the angel out of the marble. To do so, I'm going to be referencing Byung Chul-han's book, The Burnout Society which was introduced last week.

The Mayo Clinic says that depression is a "mood disorder that causes a persistent feeling of sadness and loss of interest…You may have trouble doing normal day-to-day activities, and sometimes you may feel as if life isn't worth living". Depression notably has a vague quality to it as opposed to other negative mental diagnoses like grief and frustration, which have objects of causality attached to their emergence. This makes depression difficult to understand and even more difficult to resolve. Some people will recommend taking cold showers to trigger chemicals in the brain, nature walks to break psychological patterns, or hanging out with friends to reduce the feeling of isolation. But these solutions act as bandaids for an ailment that is far deeper within ourselves and within society.

The number of people struggle to resolve this vague issue continues to surge.

As mentioned in the previous essay, we've evolved from a disciplinary society to an achievement society, which is to say that restraint and rules have been broken down and the individual has become empowered to choose whatever they want for their life - in fact they ought to choose whatever they want for their life, without anyone else telling them what to do. Empowering the individual has its obvious benefits, but this is a complicated coin with two sides and I'm only flipping it over to look at what exists on the other side.

## The "other"

Han points out that we've always had some sort of outside battle that we're programmed to fight off. He establishes a relationship between the term "other" and "negative" to represent a point of friction that we can singularly identify as the root of issues. We fight off negativity with "positivity" which is used specifically to refer to the ability to solve conflicts: deregulation, absence of external threats, understanding and realizing what can be done.

In some time periods, the battle is immunological and a virus becomes the primary enemy that we are racing to develop new technology for. At different times, the enemy has been a foreign enemy that requires the attention of the whole community. The "other" simply represents some force that we are able to target our attention towards and develop solutions for, be it new technology or social strategies to take down an abusive power.

Despite the fact that we still need remedies for diseases - many of which are chronic - and we are still fighting in wars - many of which are far away and out of the individual's control - the "other" that we are encountering is more internal than external. Rather than a disease or an enemy that threatens us, we are fighting against internal neurological issues which include "depression, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), borderline personality disorder (BDP), and burnout".

The issues we now have are issues of positivity rather than negativity. Without an external "other" giving us a friction point to go against, we are left to perpetually create positive solutions more and more and more. Our internal neurological diseases are the results of positive action, yet we still have positive methods for resolution.

## Do more!

Don't get me wrong, I'm a sucker for David Goggins shouting at the camera as much as the next person looking for a bit of internal fire, but the message of self-improvement is a fine line that we have to walk.

The paradox of a society that encourages its members to choose how they want to live their life is that they use this freedom to enslave themselves in a cycle of achievement.

In previous societies where a higher authority was more common, there might have been less freedom, but there was also a strict sense of what was required for accomplishment and thus boundaries where one could find rest. Now, without an outside source imposing itself on us, we are left to play many different roles of the game we have to define. We are both the rule-maker of what the path to success looks like and also the person that has to play within the rules that we create. This is infinitely more freeing than having the rules self-imposed onto us, but we quickly enslave ourselves by being the perpetrator and victim at the same time.

Are you really going to tell yourself that you've achieved enough? We are all captivated by the message of "you can be greater than what you are now", because according to the vague criteria for success we often set up for ourselves, it's likely true! This cycle becomes one of addiction. Therein lies the complicated battle of positivity being fought with more positivity.

Depression began to take off when these definitions of identity, born out of a sense of duty and belonging to a larger social structure, began to fade away and all that one was left with was a plate of infinite optionality with the instructions to make the most of this freedom. The ever-ascending climb to realize one's potential is tiresome.

Without boundaries, the direction and meaning within modern life easily becomes vague and with it, the emotions we experience. As mentioned earlier, depression has no object that it's attached to for causality. Apathy, the emotion of indifference, emerges as one of the primary features of depression.

Han relates the disposition of the someone experiencing neurological sickness to muselmanner, a lifeless prisoner within a concentration camp:

> In this society of compulsion, everyone carries a work camp inside. One exploits oneself. It means that exploitation is possible even without domination. People who suffer from depression, bipolar disorder, or burnout syndrome develop the symptoms displayed by the muselmanner in concentration camps. Muselmanner are emaciated prisoners lacking all vigor, who like people with acute depression, have become entirely apathetic and can no longer even recognize physical cold or the orders given by guards. One cannot help but suspect that the late-modern animal laborans [laboring animal] with neuronal disturbances would have been muselmanner, too - albeit well fed and probably obese

The dichotomy of needing to refine oneself to an end that you yourself don't really understand, yet are forced to create, has led to a generation of tired and apathetic people.

Han wraps up this point saying, "depression is the pathological expression of late-modern man's failure to become himself".

## You-sickness

With a focus entirely on yourself, the outside world becomes a means for consumption on your road to self-improvement. This is the inception of narcissistic tendencies.

As we have severed our attachment to an organized social structure that used to have defined roles and a reliance upon its members for the greater good, the aim for our individual efforts turns to the elevation of yourself. As the picture above says, you are the main character. Detached from a community, and a shared sense of unity (typically established by a negative "other" mentioned at the beginning of this), you become the project.

Relationships are relevant only in relation to your stage of your professional development. It's common to outgrow friends rather than to see difference as a natural process of our collective growth. In the digital world, followers are not friends, but instead commodities that offer us more validation than comfort. Even though I'm writing this out of a sincere desire to know more, I'm not immune to my own narcissistic temptations for others to approve of it.

In becoming obsessed on self-improvement, we become hyper-fixated on all opportunities for positive enhancement.

When the value of life is stripped down to self-enhancement, all that we have left to show for it is a worship of sleep scores and recovery days.

## Where is your nasal strip?

It's all fun and games until your health routine is out of whack and your life structure begins to falter.

Granted, it's not fair to say that all health optimizers are wrong for wanting to be healthy. It's important to want to be healthy but it's merely one aspect of our satisfaction and happiness.

The trouble comes in when we obsess over ourselves and make our physical well-being the primary focus of our days, which becomes a shallow existence. When life is reduced from something greater to serve and participate in, self-preservation is the primary objective. Worshiping health metrics is what's leftover for this sort of hallowed out experience. In the final words of The Burnout Society:

> They cannot be killed at all. Their life equals that of the undead. They are too alive to die, and too dead to live.

This, I hope, was a clear introduction to the issue of self-improvement culture that we find ourselves in today. It's important to point out because at face value, these efforts to become better and more optimized seem to be well-intentioned. But clearly we are left with a generation of people who are unhappy, to say the least.
